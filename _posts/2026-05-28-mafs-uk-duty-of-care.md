The fallout from Channel 4's *Married at First Sight UK* has dominated headlines this week, but behind the drama lies a serious legal story. Two former cast members have alleged they were raped by their on-screen partners, episodes have been pulled from streaming, and a major sponsor has walked away. Former crew members are now speaking out about a production culture they describe as "toxic from the top down." So what does the law actually say about all of this?

## Duty of Care — What Is It?

The phrase "duty of care" gets thrown around a lot, but it has a precise legal meaning. In tort law, a duty of care arises where one party is in a position of responsibility over another and it is reasonably foreseeable that careless conduct could cause harm. The landmark case is *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932], where Lord Atkin established the "neighbour principle". You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions that you could reasonably foresee would injure people closely affected by your conduct.

In the context of MAFS UK, the production company CPL sits in a clear position of power over its contestants. It controls their environment, controls who they are paired with, controls access to alcohol, and profits from their emotional vulnerability. That is precisely the kind of relationship where a duty of care arises. Former workers allege that cast members were plied with alcohol at dinner parties and that producers deliberately manufactured confrontations. If true, that is not just bad ethics, it is potentially a breach of that duty.

## Consent and Sexual Autonomy

The consent issues raised are even more serious. Two former cast members have alleged rape by their on-screen partners, allegations that are denied. But the wider picture painted by crew members is troubling in its own right. Senior producers allegedly told staff "we want them to sleep together," and bets were reportedly placed on which couples would have sex first.

Consent in law requires that it be freely given, informed, and capable of being withdrawn at any time. The Sexual Offences Act 2003 makes clear that consent obtained through pressure, manipulation, or a climate of coercion is no consent at all. While the production company is not directly accused of the alleged assaults, the question of whether the environment they created made genuine free consent harder to exercise is a legitimate legal and ethical one. Placing contestants in an isolated, controlled setting under camera, under social pressure, paired with a stranger. Raises real questions about whether the conditions for meaningful consent were ever properly protected.

## Why This Matters

CPL's lawyers argue that welfare procedures were followed and that contributors were not pressured. Channel 4 has commissioned an external review. But the law does not just ask whether rules were technically followed: it asks whether reasonable care was taken in practice.

Reality TV has long pushed the boundaries of what is acceptable in pursuit of entertainment. MAFS UK suggests those boundaries may have been crossed entirely. When the cameras roll, the law does not switch off.
